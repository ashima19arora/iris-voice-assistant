Learning. Paying bills. Booking a government appointment. Filing a complaint. Ordering what you need. All of it used to happen in a physical world, with physical adaptations — a form someone could read aloud to you, a counter you could walk up to and ask.

Everything moved onto a screen that assumes you can see it, read it, and click precisely — fast enough to keep up with a form that times out. For millions of people, the train didn't stop for them.

For the people who can't keep up, the alternative isn't a workaround. It's standing in long lines. Asking a stranger to read a form meant to be private. Handing over your bank details, your medical report, and hoping they get it right.

The legal right to access already exists — the RPWD Act 2016 requires reasonable accommodation, and GIGW 3.0 sets the accessibility standard for government platforms. Enforcement is weak: the number of accessible central government websites has been stuck at 95 since 2020.

Iris is a voice-first assistant that sits at the OS level — not inside one browser tab, but across the whole desktop. It listens for a spoken wake phrase, takes a command, and executes it: opening apps, navigating sites, filling out forms, completing multi-step tasks. It narrates what it's doing as it works, and checks in before doing anything that can't be undone.

To make specific, high-friction tasks genuinely usable — not just "open this app" — Iris includes pre-built task templates for services people actually struggle with: booking a government appointment, paying a bill, navigating a course platform. Instead of stringing together a dozen small commands, one spoken request walks through the whole flow.

Existing tools weren't built for this. Screen-reader-paired software assumes English fluency and costs real money — JAWS runs $95/year and up, over $1,000 for a perpetual license. Windows' own voice control is English-only and needs internet to work. None of them check in before acting, or admit when something went wrong.

Iris is different by design:

✓ confirmation before anything irreversible
✓ narrates what it's doing, out loud, as it works
✓ task templates for the services people actually get stuck on
✓ offline — no internet required
✓ free
✓ OS-level — the whole desktop, not just one browser tab

No one stands in line to ask a stranger to read their appointment letter aloud. No one hands their phone to someone else to check if a bill got paid. You ask Iris to book the appointment, and it books it. You ask it to check the bill, and it tells you.

The world will keep moving fast. This time, it doesn't have to leave anyone standing on the platform.

"We didn't build Iris out of standard cloud APIs. When your interface is built for people who cannot verify if a black-box model got it right, every layer of the stack must be local, deterministic, and inspectable."

// PAGE 02 — SPEECH-TO-TEXT

NVIDIA Parakeet TDT 0.6B (int8), via onnx-asr

Chosen because Whisper loaded too slowly for instant voice commands (~1.2s delay) and Vosk failed on complex Indian-English prosody. Parakeet delivers ~50ms local transcription latency on standard CPU hardware with zero data leaving the device.

most assistive tech gets built for disabled and underserved users instead of with them — decisions made from guesswork, user input arriving late or reduced to validation instead of real co-creation. it's a well-documented failure pattern: 29-35% of assistive devices get abandoned entirely. we checked every target group against real research and real prevalence data before writing a single line of code, specifically so this wasn't guesswork.
